Learn Obsidian with Phoebe · Session 5 of 6

What the graph view is (and is not) telling you

The graph view is the most screenshotted thing in Obsidian, and the most misread. It draws exactly two things, notes and the links between them, and lets physics decide where they sit. Tonight we read it the way the help describes it: what a circle, a line and a big node actually mean, what the local graph shows at depth 1 and depth 2, and why the session 4 anti-lever produced the most beautiful graph on the bench and the worst neighbourhood. By the end you can look at your own graph and say what it proves, and what it cannot.

Underneath it, one habit: treat the graph as a drawing of links, never as a score.

Neighbours on the shelf. The graph view is not a mind map: it is drawn by the app from your links, while a radial map is drawn by you from a centre, which is Mind Mapping ↗. How often you review and tidy notes in any tool is PKM ↗. Reading what the links you already have are saying is the subject here.

Part 1 · covers what the global graph encodes

Circles, lines and a lot of physics

5 min live

The help is short and precise about the graph view: "Circles represent notes" and "Lines represent internal links". The more notes reference a note, the bigger its circle. That is the whole encoding. Everything else on the screen, the clusters, the gaps, the pleasing spiral, comes from a physics simulation that pushes circles apart and pulls linked ones together.

LiveThree things the picture encodes3 min▶

Read the graph with three questions and nothing else. Which notes exist? Every circle is a note. Which notes are joined? Every line is an internal link, the same wikilinks from session 1, read from the metadata cache that the help says powers the graph view. Which notes do many others point at? Bigger circles have more notes referencing them. Hover over a circle and its connections light up while the rest fades, which is the quickest way to see one note's neighbourhood in the whole vault.

Notice what is missing. Size counts links in; it does not know whether those links came from twelve thoughtful insight notes or twelve daily notes that each mention a client name. A daily note that says "Corvid call" every morning can grow a client note into the biggest circle on the screen. Big means referenced, not important.

Self-studyForces are a layout, not a finding2 min▶

The graph settings include four forces: center pulls everything toward the middle, repel pushes circles apart, link force pulls linked circles together, and link distance sets how long a line wants to be. Change them and the same vault becomes a tight ball, a loose cloud or a spiral. Nothing in your notes changed. A cluster that "emerges" is often just a set of notes that share one hub, and turning repel up will scatter it again.

The settings also offer a time-lapse animation that adds notes in the order they were created. It is a pleasant way to watch a vault grow, and a fair record of when you wrote things. It is not a record of when ideas connected: a link written today to a note from last year appears with the older note. Menu names move between releases, so check the current help for where these settings sit.

Real world

The cluster that was a client name. A familiar pattern: a researcher spots a dense knot in their graph, assumes it is a theme they had not noticed, and spends an evening on it. Hovering shows the knot is every note that mentions one client, held together by the client note in the middle. The graph was right about the links. The reading added a meaning the links never carried.

Part 2 · covers the local graph and depth

Two steps out from the note you have open

5 min live

The global graph shows the whole vault at once, which is why it mostly looks like weather. The local graph shows only the notes connected to the active note, and a depth slider decides how far out it reaches. The help's wording is exact: "Each level of depth will show notes connected to the notes revealed at the previous depth." Depth 2 is the neighbourhood the session 4 bench measured.

LiveThe depth slider is the bench, drawn3 min▶

Open the local graph for an insight note at depth 1 and you see its direct neighbours: the notes it links to and the notes that link to it. Move the slider to 2 and each of those neighbours brings its own neighbours in. That is the same two-hop walk the session 4 bench ran for twelve questions. On rung 4, with maps of content, it reached 23.0 notes on average within two steps, and 66.6% of what it reached was on the question's theme (modelled, computed on a constructed vault).

The figure shows why. A map at depth 1 is a door: one step further and the whole theme is on screen. A daily note at depth 1 is also a door, into everything else that happened that day. Depth 2 is worth reading precisely because it shows you which of your notes are doors to where. Depth 3 usually reaches most of a small vault, which tells you very little.

LiveFilters and groups: deciding what to look at2 min▶

The help lists the graph's filters: Search files (show only notes matching a search), Tags and Attachments (show them as their own circles, or not), Existing files only (hide links to notes that do not exist yet) and Orphans ("notes without any links"). Groups paint every note matching a search term in a colour you choose, so path:Insights can be one colour and path:Daily another. The search operators are the ones from session 3.

Filters change what is drawn, not what exists. Hiding daily notes does not remove their links from the vault; it lets you see what the rest of the structure looks like without them. That comparison, with and without, is often the most informative thing the graph can show you.

Self-studyWhy the local graph beats the global one for work2 min▶

Ines never asks "what does my whole vault look like?" while working. She asks "what else do I have on notification fatigue?" with one note open. The local graph answers the second question; the global graph answers the first. Pin the local graph to the sidebar and it follows whichever note is active, so every note you open shows its own neighbourhood. Treat the global graph as an occasional audit view, mainly for orphans and islands.

Read depth 2 from the outside in. Look at the ring of depth 2 notes first and ask which depth 1 note brought each one in. A map that brings in its theme is doing its job; a daily note that brings in twelve strangers is the reason your neighbourhood feels noisy.

Part 3 · covers "the graph view is your second brain"

A rounder graph can be a worse vault

5 min live

The help says what the graph draws: notes and links. It does not say which links are relevant. Session 4's anti-lever, "link every mention of every term", made Ines's vault look perfect in every way a graph can draw and left the neighbourhood around each note mostly noise. Both camps in this disagreement are looking at the same picture; they are asking it different questions.

LiveThe anti-lever, read as a graph3 min▶

Session 4 linked every mention of every term across Ines's vault. On rung 4, links went from 498 to 1,488. Every count a graph can draw improved: orphans fell from 5 to 0, the largest connected component grew from 96.4% to 100.0%, and recall within two steps rose from 84.4% to 100.0%. Then the cost: the two-step neighbourhood grew from 23.0 notes to 181.8, nearly the whole vault, and precision fell from 66.6% to 9.1% (modelled, computed on a constructed vault). Roughly nine in ten of the notes around any note were off its theme.

As a picture, the anti-lever graph is the one people screenshot: dense, round, no stragglers. As a tool for thinking, it is the one where depth 2 shows you everything and therefore tells you nothing. A denser, rounder graph can be a worse vault, and nothing in the picture warns you.

LiveAn orphan is a question, not a defect2 min▶

The Orphans filter shows "notes without any links". The anti-lever drove that count to zero and made the vault worse, so zero orphans is not a target. Treat each orphan as a question with three honest answers. Link it up if it belongs to a theme, usually by adding it to that theme's map. Merge it if it repeats a note you already have. Leave it if it is a stray thought that has not found its place yet; Luhmann himself wrote that "some things will get lost".

On rung 4, Ines's one unanswered bench question started from an interview she never processed: it had no link up to its map. That is the orphan worth finding. The note she jotted about a conference coffee queue is not.

Self-studyWhat a graph can show: hubs and islands3 min▶

Johannes Schmidt, who coordinates the research project on Luhmann's card index, describes two features of the real archive (Schmidt 2018, read at source). Hubs are cards with an above-average number of links that give access to large parts of the file. Black holes are poorly linked parts that "tend to remain isolated later on and hence fade away"; some parts seemed untouched since they were written. A graph view is good at showing exactly these two things: the crossroads, as big circles, and the islands, as small clusters or single dots off on their own.

That is the honest use. The graph can point at where your structure is thin or where everything routes through one note. It cannot tell you whether the links are any good. Andy Matuschak warns that most people end up, after a year, with "a pile of dissociated notes" (read at source); a graph full of links can hide the same pile if the links do not say why two notes belong together.

Self-studyThe disagreement, as it stands2 min▶

For "the graph is your second brain": it is the only view that shows the whole network at once, and it makes isolated notes and over-loaded hubs visible at a glance, which no list of files can. Against: the help says it draws notes and links and nothing about relevance; the bench shows a vault can reach 0 orphans and one component while a two-step neighbourhood is mostly noise; and position, the most striking feature of the picture, comes from forces you can retune.

No controlled study was found showing that graph views, or linked notes in general, improve retrieval or learning. That leaves the question open, not settled either way. What you can do is measure your own vault, as the build-along below does.

Real world

The graph screenshot as a progress report. A familiar pattern in note-taking communities: someone posts their graph after a year, a glowing nebula of thousands of dots, and the replies admire how much they have built. The picture shows how many notes and links exist. It cannot show whether any question gets answered faster than it did a year ago, and the person posting usually knows the answer to that better than the graph does.

Build-along

Read one local graph, then audit the whole

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Work in your own vault. Ines's notes are the worked example: her insight note "Drop-off clusters at the first form that asks for an ID number", her folders Clients/Corvid/Interviews, Reading, Insights, Daily and Admin, and a map called "Notification fatigue (map)". The graph view is a core plugin, so nothing needs installing, and no community plugin is needed for any step. Counts from your vault will be your own: your numbers will differ.

Open the local graph for one insight note. Pick an insight note you know well and open its local graph (in the command palette, search for "local graph"; check the current help if the command name has moved). Set depth to 1 and write down what you see, then move it to 2.

★ Step 1 · Ines's insight note, depth 1 then 2Open note Drop-off clusters at the first form that asks for an ID number Depth 1 the interviews that cite it, the reading note, its map Depth 2 the map brings in the rest of its theme the interviews bring in their daily notes Write down how many notes at each depth (your numbers will differ)

Turn on Orphans in the global graph and list five. Open the global graph, open its filters, and make sure Orphans is switched on so notes without any links are drawn. Hover the loose dots and list five. Next to each, write one of three decisions.

★ Step 2 · five orphans, five decisionsOrphan Decision 2026-09-22 Brightwell interview P04 link up: add to its theme's map Onboarding checklist idea merge: repeats an insight note Conference coffee queue leave: a stray thought ... ... (constructed examples; your orphans and count will differ)

Create groups by search term and quiet the extras. In the graph settings, add a group for insight notes and another for daily notes, and pick a colour for each on screen: a strong one for insights, a muted one for daily notes. Switch off Tags and Attachments in the filters so the picture shows notes and links only.

★ Step 3 · groups and filtersGroups path:Insights a strong colour you pick path:Daily a muted colour you pick Filters Tags off Attachments off Orphans on

Filter out the suspected noise and compare. Type a search term into the graph's Search files filter that excludes the daily notes. Watch what disappears. If a cluster dissolves, it was being held together by daily notes, not by ideas. Search syntax changes now and then, so check the current help if the minus sign does not exclude as expected.

★ Step 4 · Search files filter-path:Daily Before one dense knot around a client note After the knot thins out; the theme maps stand clear (what you see will differ: note what vanished)

Read node size honestly. Clear the filter. Find the biggest circle and hover it. Then open that note's backlinks panel from session 1 and look at where the links come from.

★ Step 5 · whose links made it big?Biggest node ____________________ Kind of note map / insight / client / daily / other Most links from insight notes / interviews / daily notes Verdict a door to a theme, or a name everyone mentions?

Write the finding as three sentences. One sentence on what the local graph showed at depth 2, one on what the orphans were, one on what made the biggest node big. Put it in a note in your vault so you can compare next month.

★ Step 6 · the finding, Ines's version (constructed)At depth 2 my insight note reaches its whole theme through the map, plus every note from the two days I wrote the interviews. Of five orphans, one was an unprocessed interview that belonged in a map; the other four can wait. My biggest node is the Corvid client note, made big by daily notes, not ideas. (your numbers and your notes will differ)

Save the settings as a view you trust. Once your groups and filters show insights clearly and daily notes quietly, keep them. A graph you read the same way each month is comparable; a graph you retune every time only ever shows today's layout.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min
  1. Pin the local graph to the sidebar for a week, set at depth 2, and note twice when it showed you a note you had forgotten about.
  2. Act on your five orphans from step 2: link up the ones that belong, merge the repeats, and leave the rest with a one-line reason.
  3. Find one island: a small cluster that never connects to a map. Decide whether it is a theme that needs a map or a side topic that is fine on its own.
  4. Change one force (repel or link distance) as far as it goes, screenshot before and after, and write one sentence on what changed in the picture and what changed in the vault.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · In Ines's graph one circle is much bigger than the rest. What does that tell her?

The help says more referencing notes make a node bigger. Size is a count of links in, not a judgement of importance: a client note mentioned in every daily note can be the biggest circle in the vault.

2 · After linking every mention of every term, Ines's vault has 0 orphans and one connected component. Why is that not proof of a good vault?

On the session 4 bench the anti-lever raised recall to 100.0% and reached 181.8 notes in two steps, but only 9.1% of them were on the question's theme, against 66.6% on rung 4 (modelled, computed on a constructed vault). A rounder graph drew more links, not better ones.

3 · What does the local graph show at depth 2?

Each level of depth shows notes connected to the notes revealed at the previous depth. Depth 1 is the direct neighbours, both directions; depth 2 adds their neighbours, the same two-hop walk the session 4 bench measured.
