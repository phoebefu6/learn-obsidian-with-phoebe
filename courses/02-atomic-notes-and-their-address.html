Atomic notes and the address they keep

A link is only as good as the thing it points at. Tonight we open the most famous note system in the literature, Niklas Luhmann's slip box, and read what it actually was: a fixed number on every card, so any card could be linked to and branched from without ever being moved. Then we look hard at the claim that the box made him productive, and find one self-reported case that nobody can untangle from the man. Finally we take the part that does travel, one idea per note with a title that says the idea, and split a fat interview note into three atomic insight notes that link back to the exact sentence they came from.

Part 1 · covers Luhmann's slip box as it actually was

A number that never moves

5 min live

In 1981 the sociologist Niklas Luhmann wrote a short essay, "Communicating with Slip Boxes", which he called "a piece of empirical sociology" about himself and his slip box (read at source, in Manfred Kuehn's translation). By then they had worked together for "more than twenty-six years". The archive that holds the box counts about 90,000 handwritten cards: Schmidt (2018) gives approximately 90,000, and the German academies' union says more than 90,000, kept over 40 years. Older retellings say 70,000; use "about 90,000". What made the box work was not the cards but the number written on each one.

LiveA fixed place, not a topic3 min▶

Luhmann writes that he decided "against the systematic ordering in accordance with topics and sub-topics" and chose "a firm fixed place" instead. His line is worth remembering: "The fixed filing place needs no system." Every slip got a number when it was filed, and that number never changed.

The number is what made growth cheap. Slip 57/12 is continued by 57/13. If a word on 57/12 sparks a side thought, it becomes 57/12a, then 57/12b, filed right behind it; a thought on 57/12a becomes 57/12a1. Any slip can branch at any point, forever, and nothing already in the drawer is disturbed.

Because numbers never move, you can "add as many references to them as you may want", and he recorded "back links in the slips that are being linked to". With no topical order to browse, he needed a keyword register, constantly updated, to find a way in. And he was blunt about the price of isolation: "A note that is not connected to this network will get lost."

Self-studyWhat Obsidian keeps and what it drops3 min▶

In Obsidian the address of a note is its title: a wikilink points at a name. That keeps the most useful half of Luhmann's idea (any note can be linked to from anywhere, and backlinks are worked out for you) and drops the other half. His number never changed. A title can. Rename a note inside Obsidian and its links are updated for you; rename it somewhere else and they break. Session 6 handles renaming safely.

If you want an address that never says anything about the content, the Unique note creator core plugin makes notes with time-coded titles, the closest thing in Obsidian to a fixed number. Most people prefer claim titles (Part 3), which read well inside a sentence but must be chosen with care, because changing them is a rename.

One thing Obsidian adds that paper could not: links can point inside a note, at a heading or a single block. Part 3 uses that to link at a sentence rather than a whole file.

"No system" did not mean no structure. The box still had a few broad top-level areas; what Luhmann refused was a tree of topics inside them. Session 3 reads the record on this, because it is the strongest argument for maps of content over folders.

Part 2 · covers the claim that the box made Luhmann productive

One case, and the people who disagree about it

5 min live

Almost every note-taking pitch you will meet says some version of "Luhmann wrote a mountain of books because of his slip box". It is worth knowing exactly what supports that sentence, because the evidence is real, interesting and much thinner than the pitch. The honest position is a disagreement, and it stays one.

LiveWhat the record supports3 min▶

For. Luhmann himself describes the box as a "partner of communication" and says every note gets its quality "only from the network of links and back-links" (read). The media scholar Markus Krajewski is reported by Deutschlandfunk Kultur as calling the box the "motor" of Luhmann's text production (reported). Sonke Ahrens, who teaches the method, frames Luhmann's output as the box's result, and zettelkasten.de says the method "amplifies your effectiveness" (both advocates, read at source).

Against, or limiting. It is one case, described by its own user: Luhmann called the essay an empirical account of himself and his box. He also says the box "needs a number of years in order to reach critical mass" and until then "functions as a mere container", and that theoretical publications "do therefore not result from simply copying" what is in it (read). Johannes Schmidt, who coordinates the archive project, writes that its creativity "was not totally independent from its creator of course" (read). Training, talent and decades of work sit in the same single case, and with one case they cannot be pulled apart.

Notice what the advocates say themselves: zettelkasten.de adds that the gain comes "not with less effort per time". That is a much smaller claim than the pitch.

Self-studyWhy the counts disagree2 min▶

Three sources, three numbers. Krajewski is reported as saying "around 50 books and 600 articles". Ahrens's author page says Luhmann published "roughly another 60 books and hundreds of articles" in 29 and a half years. Schmidt (2018) says his publication list at his death "comprised more than 500 titles". They are counting different things (books, articles, all titles, a time window) and at least one comes to us second-hand.

The cards have the same problem in miniature: about 90,000 in Schmidt and the academies' page, 70,000 in older secondary retellings. The professional habit is to quote the range with its source and tier, and to say "about". If a number matters to your argument and the sources disagree, the disagreement is the finding.

Real world

The method borrowed from a famous story. A familiar pattern: a team adopts a note system because someone famous used it, spends a month on numbering schemes and folder rules, and then wonders why nothing feels smarter. The story told them what the famous person did, not what made the difference, and the original author said the box was a mere container for years. Borrow the mechanism you can test on your own notes, and drop the promise.

No controlled study was found. Nothing read for these sessions tests whether linked, atomic notes improve learning or output against a comparison group. Treat every "this will make you more productive" as a hypothesis about you, and check it against your own vault.

Part 3 · covers atomic notes, claim titles, block links and embeds

One idea per note, and a title that says it

5 min live

The part of Luhmann that travels is small and testable: a note that holds one idea can be linked to precisely, and a note that holds five cannot. Andy Matuschak's working notes (read at source) put it as principles: evergreen notes should be atomic, concept-oriented and densely linked, "written and organized to evolve, contribute, and accumulate over time, across projects". Obsidian then lets you link not just to a note but to one sentence inside it.

LiveAtomic, concept-oriented, titled as a claim3 min▶

Matuschak's rule for atomic notes: "only about one thing - but which, as much as possible, capture the entirety of that thing". Too broad and every link to the note is vague; too fragmented and the network breaks into crumbs. He admits "There's no clear litmus test", which is honest and useful: you judge it by whether a link to the note would mean one thing.

Concept-oriented means factoring notes by idea "rather than by author, book, event, project, topic". An interview is an event, so an interview note is fine as a record, but the ideas inside it deserve notes of their own that can collect evidence from many interviews and clients.

Titles do the most work. "Evergreen note titles are like APIs", he writes, and he prefers complete phrases. "Trust" is a topic; "Trust is borrowed from the brand people already use" is a claim. Every link to a claim title reads as a sentence in the note that contains it, and you can disagree with it, which you cannot do with a topic.

LiveBlock links, heading links, embeds and display text2 min▶

A wikilink can point inside a note. [[Note#Heading]] links to a heading; [[# starts a heading link in the same note, and [[## term searches headings across the vault. [[Note#^37066d]] links to a single block, a paragraph or list item. Obsidian can generate the id, or you can write your own; the help says custom block ids "can only consist of Latin letters, numbers, and dashes".

Put ! in front of any internal link and it becomes an embed: ![[Note#^id]] shows that block inside the current note, and embedded content stays up to date when the source changes. Display text, [[Example|Custom name]], changes the words a link shows without changing where it points; for a name you will reuse, use an alias (session 1).

One limit to say out loud: the help states that block references "won't work outside of Obsidian". In another editor, ^p07-fee is just text at the end of a paragraph. Use block links where precision pays, not on every sentence.

Self-studyNote types: interview, reading, insight3 min▶

Ines keeps three kinds of note, and the kind decides the title. An interview note is a record of an event, titled by date, client and participant ("2026-04-11 Brightwell interview P07"); it is never rewritten after the week it happened. A reading note records what a source says, titled by the source. An insight note holds one idea of hers, titled as a claim, and grows as evidence arrives from many records.

Sonke Ahrens's How to Take Smart Notes (2017) names a similar split, fleeting, literature and permanent notes (reported: the book was not read for these sessions). Matuschak observes that most people take only transient notes and "use notes as a bucket for storage or scratch thoughts", and that after a year they hold "a pile of dissociated notes". His practice is "heavily inspired by Niklas Luhmann's Zettelkasten", though he uses a different term on purpose.

How you read a text and turn it into a reading note by recall belongs to Reading ↗. Here a reading note is simply one note type with its own title rule.

Self-studyTemplates: start every insight the same way2 min▶

The Templates core plugin inserts a note you have written as a template. Three variables are filled in on insert: {{title}} (the note's title), {{date}} (YYYY-MM-DD by default) and {{time}} (HH:mm by default); both date and time accept Moment.js format strings. You point the plugin at a templates folder in its settings; check the current help for the exact setting name, because menus change between releases.

Templater and similar community plugins do much more, and are worth a look once the core plugin feels limiting. Nothing in these sessions needs them.

Real world

The note called "Meeting notes 3". A familiar sight in any shared drive: dozens of notes titled by date or meeting name, each holding a decision, two complaints and an idea. Searching finds them, but linking to one says nothing about which part mattered. Pull the one recurring idea into a note titled as a claim and it can start collecting evidence from meetings nobody remembered were related.

Build-along

Split Ines's fattest interview note

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Take the longest note in your vault (homework from session 1 found it) or follow Ines's interview with participant P07 at Brightwell Mutual, constructed for the purpose. You will leave the interview as an untouched record, pull each idea into its own insight note, and link back to the exact sentence each insight rests on.

Pick your fattest note and count its ideas. Open it and write, at the bottom, one line per separate idea. An idea is something you could imagine a different note linking to on its own. Ines's interview has three.

★ Step 1 · 2026-04-11 Brightwell interview P07.md (the end of it)P07, renewal journey, video call. Only trusted the form "because it looked like my bank". Asked why we need her employer: "felt like they were checking up on me." "Then there was a fee on the last screen. That's when I stopped believing anything they said." Ideas in here: 3 (trust, data requests, the fee)

Write the first insight as a claim title. Create a new note whose title is the idea stated as something that could be wrong. Write two or three lines in your own words, and link back to the interview inside a sentence.

★ Step 2 · Trust is borrowed from the brand people already use.mdPeople extend trust to a new form when it looks like something they already trust, such as their bank's app. First seen in [[2026-04-11 Brightwell interview P07]]. Next two titles, same pattern: Unexplained data requests read as a threat A surprise fee at checkout reads as dishonesty

Give the fee quote a block id and link to it. In the interview note, go to the end of the fee quote's paragraph and type a space, a caret and an id. Ids may use only Latin letters, numbers and dashes. Then, in the fee insight, link to that exact paragraph.

★ Step 3 · a block id in the interview, a block link in the insightIn 2026-04-11 Brightwell interview P07: "Then there was a fee on the last screen. That's when I stopped believing anything they said." ^p07-fee In A surprise fee at checkout reads as dishonesty: P07 said it outright: [[2026-04-11 Brightwell interview P07#^p07-fee|P07 on the fee]].

Embed the quote in the insight. Add a line with the same link and a ! in front. Reading view now shows P07's own words inside the insight. Edit the quote in the interview and the embed updates; the words are stored once.

★ Step 4 · A surprise fee at checkout reads as dishonesty.mdA fee that appears only at the last step makes people re-read everything before it as a trick. Evidence: ![[2026-04-11 Brightwell interview P07#^p07-fee]] (Outside Obsidian this line stays plain text: block references do not work in other editors.)

Make an "Insight note" template. Create a folder called Templates, add a note named "Insight note" with the text below, and set that folder as the template folder in the Templates core plugin's settings (check the current help for the setting's exact name). Next time, create a note, title it as a claim, and insert the template.

★ Step 5 · Templates/Insight note.md--- type: insight created: {{date}} --- # {{title}} Claim in two lines, in my own words. Evidence: - [[record#^block-id]] Related:

Count what changed. Open the interview note's backlinks panel: each insight now appears under Linked mentions. Write the before and after counts in your homework file. Ines's counts are constructed; yours come from your own note.

★ Step 6 · the finding (your numbers will differ)Before 1 note, 3 ideas, 0 links After 4 notes: 1 interview (unchanged record + 1 block id) 3 insights, each titled as a claim 3 links back to the interview, 1 of them to a block 1 embed of the fee quote Backlinks on the interview: 3 (worked out by Obsidian)

Leave the record alone. The interview stays what happened, in the order it happened. Splitting moves your ideas out, not the evidence: if a client ever asks "where did that come from?", every insight points to the exact sentence.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What did Luhmann's fixed, never-changing slip numbers give him?

57/12 could gain 57/12a, 57/12b and 57/12a1 years later, filed behind it, and any slip could be referenced by number from anywhere. He chose a fixed place over a topic system, and he warned that an unconnected note "will get lost", so the numbers guaranteed nothing on their own.

2 · What is the strongest honest statement about the slip box and Luhmann's output?

Luhmann's account and the advocates argue for the box; Luhmann himself calls it "a mere container" for years, and the archive's coordinator says its creativity was "not totally independent from its creator". One case cannot separate the two, and no controlled study was found. C overstates the other side: "a mere container" was about the early years.

3 · Which title makes the best atomic insight note?

D states one idea as a claim, so every link to it reads as a sentence and the note can collect evidence from any client. A is a project, B a topic and C an event, which is exactly how Matuschak says not to factor notes.
